He says this early on during his latest one-man show, Latin History for Morons. The 52-year-old actor was born in Colombia, but moved to New York when he was only 4 years old. He grew up in Queens and studied theater at NYU, before dropping out and starting what would become a prolific career in acting: He’s acted in more than 100 movies and TV shows and his one-man shows, like Freak and Ghetto Klown, have gone on to become HBO specials.Leguizamo is the embodiment of the American dream: the young immigrant from the ghetto who goes on to be a Hollywood star. And yet, he frequently finds himself under attack, mostly on Twitter, because of his heritage. “People will say something like, ‘Why don’t you get out of our country if you don’t like it?’ or ‘You don’t belong here,’ ” he shares. A few years ago, he found out his teenage son was being bullied in high school in similar ways. He set out to educate himself on the many heroes of Latino culture to help his son feel proud of their background. That experience birthed his latest show, Latin History for Morons, a 95-minute SparkNotes-style session highlighting everything from the Aztecs and Incas all the way up to Pitbull.Aside from having more than a few laugh-out-loud moments, Leguizamo’s lesson is full of facts that would serve many well, including our wall-builder in chief. For instance, did you know we have Latinos to thank for bringing corn, tomatoes, and chocolate into the United States? Or that Latinos—numbering in the hundreds of thousands—fought in every major war in American history? As Leguizamo put it: “If in my son’s class they would have read all the Latin contributions, people wouldn’t be so ready to attack us.” We spoke to the actor over the phone about his new play and why Hollywood needs to start changing the way it thinks about Hispanic actors, too.When was the exact moment you realized you had to do this show?I guess my whole life has been preparing me for this show in a lot of ways. I’ve had a lot of those fights growing up, even now on Twitter. And then when my son got bullied, I couldn’t believe it was still going on, even for my kids. Were you already working on it back then?I’ve been working on this show for four years. These shows take me years; Ghetto Klown was 10 years. They’re very difficult and complicated because I have very little time and it’s only one person to tell one story.Latin history is a pretty broad subject. How did you decide what to focus on?It wasn’t too hard because of the comments that you get. [They’ll say,] “What have Latin people done that’s so great?” So I had to go back to our empires. In school, the things that you study are the wars, more than anything else in history. I wanted to address the very issues that people try to hurt us with.What was your experience like attending high school in New York? Were you exposed to any Latino history at all?Nonexistent. There was a little bit of improvement in my son’s education, but Latin and black contributions—and I don’t mean to lump us together—were nonexistent in my public school. Even when I went to college, there was nothing. When I was studying the Civil War, there was nothing about everything we did, not one mention of any participation or contribution, ever. And it would’ve changed my life.When I went to see the show, I noticed it was mostly a white crowd in the audience. Was this the case in most of the shows? I have to say, I was surprised by it.It’s not usually my audience, but because they allow all the subscribers [of the Public Theater] to get the tickets first, they bought them all. But I’m still getting a lot of Latin groups. It’s not my usual audience; my usual audience is 60 percent Latin and black. Right now the mix is the opposite. But you know what’s fascinating? The crew that’s working on the show are asking me, “John, what book should I read first? I really want to start looking into Latin history.” And none of them are Latin. So that was very beautiful.What kind of books are you recommending?I’d say there are three books: Howard Zinn’s A People’s History of the United States, because it’s the easier way for Americans to understand what happened. And then I would say, Open Veins of Latin America [by Eduardo Galeano], because it’s the most researched, most beautifully written book from the Latin point of view. And then, 1491 [by Charles C. In your industry, Hollywood tends to underrepresent Latinos or villainize them as drug dealers and criminals. Why do you think that is?In Hollywood, things have improved immensely. Behind the camera, especially. There are so many Latin directors of photography, producers, first assistant directors, second ADs. Behind the scenes, we’re really moving up much faster. In front of the camera, there’s still a lot of these stories and these characters that are all still slightly negative. This negative twist of things is very damaging to us, and allows people like POTUS 45 and racist groups and even our kids to see themselves in negative shades. We need a lot more positive Latin role models in movies and in television. They exist! It’s not fiction. We’re not making shit up. I go to a lot of political rallies and Latin caucuses in D.C., and you see tons of 20-year-olds, 30-year-olds, all college-educated, political, super intelligent, confident, and hundreds of them, trying to make a difference across America. Where are all these people in movies and television?You mention in your op-ed that an agent once told you: “John, you’re so talented, but too bad you’re Latin—otherwise you’d be so much further along.”He meant it in a positive way. Even back in the day, when I was pitching Latin projects, a studio head said: “Well, you know Latin people don’t really like to see Latin people. They like to see white people.” I asked him where he got that kind of research from? You just hear the craziest shit.Why do you keep doing one-man shows? You’re established in Hollywood, yet you keep coming back to the stage pretty frequently.I love the power of theater. I think it’s an experience you never forget. It’s more powerful than film or television. I know the plays I saw with Al Pacino, American Buffalo, and True West with John Malkovich, I feel like I lived those moments. It becomes a part of your memory and your life in a way that film and TV has a little bit of distance. Also, theater allows you to be much more political; it allows you to be much more who you are. Where did Hamilton happen? It didn’t happen in movies and television, because it couldn’t. “So we’re going to do a historic piece on Hamilton and everyone is going to be black and Latin playing our forefathers.” They would say: “But wait, they didn’t speak hip-hop in the 1700s?” It would’ve never happened! But where did it happen?
